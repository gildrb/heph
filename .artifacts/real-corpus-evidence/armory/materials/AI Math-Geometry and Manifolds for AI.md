A triangle immersed in a saddle-shape plane (a [hyperbolic paraboloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_paraboloid)), as well as two diverging [ultraparallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_geometry#Non-intersecting_/_parallel_lines)

**Differential geometry** is a [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics) discipline that studies the [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry) of smooth shapes and smooth spaces, otherwise known as [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold). It uses the techniques of [vector calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_calculus), [linear algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_algebra) and [multilinear algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilinear_algebra). The field has its origins in the study of [spherical geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_geometry) as far back as [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity). It also relates to [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), the [geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesy) of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth), and later the study of [hyperbolic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_geometry) by [Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Lobachevsky). The simplest examples of smooth spaces are the [plane and space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry_of_curves) and [surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry_of_surfaces) in the three-dimensional [Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space), and the study of these shapes formed the basis for development of modern differential geometry during the 18th and 19th centuries.

Since the late 19th century, differential geometry has grown into a field concerned more generally with geometric structures on [differentiable manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_manifold). A geometric structure is one which defines some notion of size, distance, shape, volume, or other rigidifying structure. For example, in [[Riemannian Geometry]] distances and angles are specified, in [symplectic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_geometry) volumes may be computed, in [conformal geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_geometry) only angles are specified, and in [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory_(mathematics) "Gauge theory (mathematics)") certain [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_field) are given over the space. Differential geometry is closely related to, and is sometimes taken to include, [differential topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_topology), which concerns itself with properties of differentiable manifolds that do not rely on any additional geometric structure (see that article for more discussion on the distinction between the two subjects). Differential geometry is also related to the geometric aspects of the theory of [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation), otherwise known as [geometric analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_analysis).

Differential geometry finds applications throughout mathematics and the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science). Most prominently the language of differential geometry was used by [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein) in his [theory of general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_general_relativity), and subsequently by [physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicists) in the development of [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory) and the [Standard Model of particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model_of_particle_physics). Outside of physics, differential geometry finds applications in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), [control theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_theory), [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics) and [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision), and recently in [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning).

## History and development

The history and development of differential geometry as a subject begins at least as far back as [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity). It is intimately linked to the development of geometry more generally, of the notion of space and shape, and of [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology), especially the study of [[Manifold|manifolds]]. In this section we focus primarily on the history of the application of [infinitesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal) methods to geometry, and later to the ideas of [[Tangent Space|tangent spaces]], and eventually the development of the modern formalism of the subject in terms of [tensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor) and [tensor fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_field).

### Classical antiquity until the Renaissance (300 BC – 1600 AD)

The study of differential geometry, or at least the study of the geometry of smooth shapes, can be traced back at least to [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity). In particular, much was known about the geometry of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth), a [spherical geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_geometry), in the time of the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) mathematicians. Famously, [Eratosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eratosthenes) calculated the [circumference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumference) of the Earth around 200 BC, and around 150 AD [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy) in his *[Geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_(Ptolemy) "Geography (Ptolemy)")* introduced the [stereographic projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereographic_projection) for the purposes of mapping the shape of the Earth. Implicitly throughout this time principles that form the foundation of differential geometry and calculus were used in [geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesy), although in a much simplified form. Namely, as far back as [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid)'s *[Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements)* it was understood that a straight line could be defined by its property of providing the shortest distance between two points, and applying this same principle to the surface of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth) leads to the conclusion that [great circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_circles), which are only locally similar to straight lines in a flat plane, provide the shortest path between two points on the Earth's surface. Indeed, the measurements of distance along such [geodesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic) paths by Eratosthenes and others can be considered a rudimentary measure of [arclength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arclength) of curves, a concept which did not see a rigorous definition in terms of calculus until the 1600s.

Around this time there were only minimal overt applications of the theory of [infinitesimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal) to the study of geometry, a precursor to the modern calculus-based study of the subject. In [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid)'s *[Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements)* the notion of [tangency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangency) of a line to a circle is discussed, and [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes) applied the [method of exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_exhaustion) to compute the areas of smooth shapes such as the [circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle), and the volumes of smooth three-dimensional solids such as the sphere, cones, and cylinders.

There was little development in the theory of differential geometry between antiquity and the beginning of the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance). Before the development of calculus by [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton) and [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz), the most significant development in the understanding of differential geometry came from [Gerardus Mercator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerardus_Mercator)'s development of the [Mercator projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercator_projection) as a way of mapping the Earth. Mercator had an understanding of the advantages and pitfalls of his map design, and in particular was aware of the [conformal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_map_projection) nature of his projection, as well as the difference between *praga*, the lines of shortest distance on the Earth, and the *directio*, the straight line paths on his map. Mercator noted that the praga were *oblique curvatur* in this projection. This fact reflects the lack of a [metric-preserving map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry) of the Earth's surface onto a flat plane, a consequence of the later [Theorema Egregium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorema_Egregium) of [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss).

### After calculus (1600–1800)

![[media/15f16dca8020818a70052d289ec72c97a5b893a1.png]]
An osculating circle of plane curve

The first systematic or rigorous treatment of geometry using the theory of infinitesimals and notions from [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus) began around the 1600s when calculus was first developed by [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) and [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton). At this time, the recent work of [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes) introducing [analytic coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_geometry) to geometry allowed geometric shapes of increasing complexity to be described rigorously. In particular around this time [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat), Newton, and Leibniz began the study of [plane curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_curve) and the investigation of concepts such as points of [inflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflection_point) and circles of [osculation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osculating_circle), which aid in the measurement of [curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature). Indeed, already in his [first paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_Methodus_pro_Maximis_et_Minimis) on the foundations of calculus, Leibniz notes that the infinitesimal condition $d^{2}y=0$ indicates the existence of an inflection point. Shortly after this time the [Bernoulli brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_family), [Jacob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Bernoulli) and [Johann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Bernoulli) made important early contributions to the use of infinitesimals to study geometry. In lectures by Johann Bernoulli at the time, later collated by [L'Hopital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillaume_de_l%27H%C3%B4pital) into [the first textbook on differential calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analyse_des_Infiniment_Petits_pour_l%27Intelligence_des_Lignes_Courbes), the tangents to plane curves of various types are computed using the condition $dy=0$, and similarly points of inflection are calculated. At this same time the [orthogonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonality) between the osculating circles of a plane curve and the tangent directions is realised, and the first analytical formula for the radius of an osculating circle, essentially the first analytical formula for the notion of [curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature), is written down.

In the wake of the development of analytic geometry and plane curves, [Alexis Clairaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Clairaut) began the study of [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve) at just the age of 16. In his book Clairaut introduced the notion of tangent and [subtangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtangent) directions to space curves in relation to the directions which lie along a [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface) on which the space curve lies. Thus Clairaut demonstrated an implicit understanding of the [[Tangent Space]] of a surface and studied this idea using calculus for the first time. Importantly Clairaut introduced the terminology of *curvature* and *double curvature*, essentially the notion of [principal curvatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_curvature) later studied by Gauss and others.

Around this same time, [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler), originally a student of Johann Bernoulli, provided many significant contributions not just to the development of geometry, but to mathematics more broadly. In regards to differential geometry, Euler studied the notion of a [geodesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic) on a surface deriving the first analytical [geodesic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_equation), and later introduced the first set of intrinsic coordinate systems on a surface, beginning the theory of *intrinsic geometry* upon which modern geometric ideas are based. Around this time Euler's study of mechanics in the *[Mechanica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanica)* lead to the realization that a mass traveling along a surface not under the effect of any force would traverse a geodesic path, an early precursor to the important foundational ideas of Einstein's [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity), and also to the [Euler–Lagrange equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%E2%80%93Lagrange_equations) and the first theory of the [calculus of variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_variations), which underpins in modern differential geometry many techniques in [symplectic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_geometry) and [geometric analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_analysis). This theory was used by [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange), a co-developer of the calculus of variations, to derive the first differential equation describing a [minimal surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_surface) in terms of the Euler–Lagrange equation. In 1760 Euler proved a theorem expressing the curvature of a space curve on a surface in terms of the principal curvatures, known as [Euler's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%27s_theorem_(differential_geometry) "Euler's theorem (differential geometry)").

Later in the 1700s, the new French school led by [Gaspard Monge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspard_Monge) began to make contributions to differential geometry. Monge made important contributions to the theory of plane curves, surfaces, and studied [surfaces of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfaces_of_revolution) and [envelopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envelope_(mathematics) "Envelope (mathematics)") of plane curves and space curves. Several students of Monge made contributions to this same theory, and for example [Charles Dupin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dupin) provided a new interpretation of Euler's theorem in terms of the principle curvatures, which is the modern form of the equation.

### Intrinsic geometry and non-Euclidean geometry (1800–1900)

The field of differential geometry became an area of study considered in its own right, distinct from the more broad idea of analytic geometry, in the 1800s, primarily through the foundational work of [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss) and [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann), and also in the important contributions of [Nikolai Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Lobachevsky) on [hyperbolic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_geometry) and [non-Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry) and throughout the same period the development of [projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry).

Dubbed the single most important work in the history of differential geometry, in 1827 Gauss produced the *Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas* detailing the general theory of curved surfaces. In this work and his subsequent papers and unpublished notes on the theory of surfaces, Gauss has been dubbed the inventor of non-Euclidean geometry and the inventor of intrinsic differential geometry. In his fundamental paper Gauss introduced the [Gauss map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss_map), [Gaussian curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_curvature), [first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_fundamental_form) and [second fundamental forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_fundamental_form), proved the [Theorema Egregium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorema_Egregium) showing the intrinsic nature of the Gaussian curvature, and studied geodesics, computing the area of a [geodesic triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_triangle) in various non-Euclidean geometries on surfaces.

At this time Gauss was already of the opinion that the standard paradigm of [Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry) should be discarded, and was in possession of private manuscripts on non-Euclidean geometry which informed his study of geodesic triangles. Around this same time [János Bolyai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A1nos_Bolyai) and Lobachevsky independently discovered [hyperbolic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_geometry) and thus demonstrated the existence of consistent geometries outside Euclid's paradigm. Concrete models of hyperbolic geometry were produced by [Eugenio Beltrami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenio_Beltrami) later in the 1860s, and [Felix Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Klein) coined the term non-Euclidean geometry in 1871, and through the [Erlangen program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen_program) put Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries on the same footing. Implicitly, the [spherical geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_geometry) of the Earth that had been studied since antiquity was a non-Euclidean geometry, an [elliptic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_geometry).

The development of intrinsic differential geometry in the language of Gauss was spurred on by his student, [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann) in his [Habilitationsschrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitationsschrift), *On the hypotheses which lie at the foundation of geometry*. In this work Riemann introduced the notion of a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) and the [Riemannian curvature tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_curvature_tensor) for the first time, and began the systematic study of differential geometry in higher dimensions. This intrinsic point of view in terms of the Riemannian metric, denoted by $ds^{2}$ by Riemann, was the development of an idea of Gauss's about the linear element $ds$ of a surface. At this time Riemann began to introduce the systematic use of [linear algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_algebra) and [multilinear algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilinear_algebra) into the subject, making great use of the theory of [quadratic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_form) in his investigation of metrics and curvature. At this time Riemann did not yet develop the modern notion of a manifold, as even the notion of a [topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space) had not been encountered, but he did propose that it might be possible to investigate or measure the properties of the metric of [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime) through the analysis of masses within spacetime, linking with the earlier observation of Euler that masses under the effect of no forces would travel along geodesics on surfaces, and predicting Einstein's fundamental observation of the [equivalence principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_principle) a full 60 years before it appeared in the scientific literature.

In the wake of Riemann's new description, the focus of techniques used to study differential geometry shifted from the ad hoc and extrinsic methods of the study of curves and surfaces to a more systematic approach in terms of [tensor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_calculus) and Klein's Erlangen program, and progress increased in the field. The notion of groups of transformations was developed by [Sophus Lie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophus_Lie) and [Jean Gaston Darboux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Gaston_Darboux), leading to important results in the theory of [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_groups) and [symplectic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_geometry). The notion of differential calculus on curved spaces was studied by [Elwin Christoffel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elwin_Christoffel), who introduced the [Christoffel symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoffel_symbols) which describe the [covariant derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_derivative) in 1868, and by others including [Eugenio Beltrami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenio_Beltrami) who studied many analytic questions on manifolds. In 1899 [Luigi Bianchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Bianchi) produced his *Lectures on differential geometry* which studied differential geometry from Riemann's perspective, and a year later [Tullio Levi-Civita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tullio_Levi-Civita) and [Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorio_Ricci-Curbastro) produced their textbook systematically developing the theory of [absolute differential calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_differential_calculus) and [tensor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_calculus). It was in this language that differential geometry was used by Einstein in the development of general relativity and [pseudo-Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian_geometry).

### Modern differential geometry (1900–2000)

The subject of modern differential geometry emerged from the early 1900s in response to the foundational contributions of many mathematicians, including importantly [the work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_Situs_(paper) "Analysis Situs (paper)") of [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincar%C3%A9) on the foundations of [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology). At the start of the 1900s there was a major movement within mathematics to formalise the foundational aspects of the subject to avoid crises of rigour and accuracy, known as [Hilbert's program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert%27s_program). As part of this broader movement, the notion of a [topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space) was distilled in by [Felix Hausdorff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Hausdorff) in 1914, and by 1942 there were many different notions of manifold of a combinatorial and differential-geometric nature.

Interest in the subject was also focused by the emergence of Einstein's theory of general relativity and the importance of the Einstein Field equations. Einstein's theory popularised the tensor calculus of Ricci and Levi-Civita and introduced the notation $g$ for a Riemannian metric, and $\Gamma$ for the Christoffel symbols, both coming from *G* in *Gravitation*. [Élie Cartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89lie_Cartan) helped reformulate the foundations of the differential geometry of smooth manifolds in terms of [exterior calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_calculus) and the theory of [moving frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moving_frames), leading in the world of physics to [Einstein–Cartan theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein%E2%80%93Cartan_theory).

Following this early development, many mathematicians contributed to the development of the modern theory, including [Jean-Louis Koszul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Louis_Koszul) who introduced [connections on vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connection_(vector_bundle) "Connection (vector bundle)"), [Shiing-Shen Chern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiing-Shen_Chern) who introduced [characteristic classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_class) to the subject and began the study of [complex manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_manifold), [Sir William Vallance Douglas Hodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._D._Hodge) and [Georges de Rham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_de_Rham) who expanded understanding of [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms), [Charles Ehresmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Ehresmann) who introduced the theory of fibre bundles and [Ehresmann connections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ehresmann_connection), and others. Of particular importance was [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl) who made important contributions to the foundations of general relativity, introduced the [Weyl tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_tensor) providing insight into [conformal geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_geometry), and first defined the notion of a [gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_(mathematics)) leading to the development of [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory) in physics and [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory_(mathematics) "Gauge theory (mathematics)").

In the middle and late 20th century differential geometry as a subject expanded in scope and developed links to other areas of mathematics and physics. The development of [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory) and [Yang–Mills theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang%E2%80%93Mills_theory) in physics brought bundles and connections into focus, leading to developments in [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory_(mathematics) "Gauge theory (mathematics)"). Many analytical results were investigated including the proof of the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah%E2%80%93Singer_index_theorem). The development of [complex geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_geometry) was spurred on by parallel results in [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry), and results in the geometry and global analysis of complex manifolds were proven by [Shing-Tung Yau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shing-Tung_Yau) and others. In the latter half of the 20th century new analytic techniques were developed in regards to curvature flows such as the [Ricci flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_flow), which culminated in [Grigori Perelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Perelman)'s proof of the [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_conjecture). During this same period primarily due to the influence of [Michael Atiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Atiyah), new links between [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics) and differential geometry were formed. Techniques from the study of the [Yang–Mills equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang%E2%80%93Mills_equations) and [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory) were used by mathematicians to develop new invariants of smooth manifolds. Physicists such as [Edward Witten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Witten), the only physicist to be awarded a [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal), made new impacts in mathematics by using [topological quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_quantum_field_theory) and [string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory) to make predictions and provide frameworks for new rigorous mathematics, which has resulted for example in the conjectural [mirror symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_symmetry_(string_theory) "Mirror symmetry (string theory)") and the [Seiberg–Witten invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiberg%E2%80%93Witten_invariant).

## Branches

### Riemannian geometry

Riemannian geometry studies [Riemannian manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_manifold), [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) with a *Riemannian metric*. This is a concept of distance expressed by means of a [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_bilinear_form) [symmetric bilinear form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_bilinear_form) defined on the tangent space at each point. Riemannian geometry generalizes [Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry) to spaces that are not necessarily flat, though they still resemble Euclidean space at each point infinitesimally, i.e. in the [first order of approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_order_of_approximation). Various concepts based on length, such as the [arc length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_length) of curves, [area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area) of plane regions, and [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume) of solids all possess natural analogues in Riemannian geometry. The notion of a [directional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directional_derivative) of a function from [[Multivariable Calculus]] is extended to the notion of a [covariant derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_derivative) of a [tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor). Many concepts of analysis and differential equations have been generalized to the setting of Riemannian manifolds.

A distance-preserving [diffeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism) between Riemannian manifolds is called an [isometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry). This notion can also be defined *locally*, i.e. for small neighborhoods of points. Any two regular curves are locally isometric. However, the [Theorema Egregium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorema_Egregium) of Carl Friedrich Gauss showed that for surfaces, the existence of a local isometry imposes that the [Gaussian curvatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_curvature) at the corresponding points must be the same. In higher dimensions, the [Riemann curvature tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_curvature_tensor) is an important pointwise invariant associated with a Riemannian manifold that measures how close it is to being flat. An important class of Riemannian manifolds is the [Riemannian symmetric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_symmetric_space), whose curvature is not necessarily constant. These are the closest analogues to the "ordinary" plane and space considered in Euclidean and [non-Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry).

### Pseudo-Riemannian geometry

[Pseudo-Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian_manifold) generalizes Riemannian geometry to the case in which the [metric tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_tensor) need not be [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_bilinear_form). A special case of this is a [Lorentzian manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold), which is the mathematical basis of Einstein's [general relativity theory of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity).

### Finsler geometry

Finsler geometry has *Finsler manifolds* as the main object of study. This is a differential manifold with a *Finsler metric*, that is, a [Banach norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_norm) defined on each tangent space. Riemannian manifolds are special cases of the more general Finsler manifolds. A Finsler structure on a manifold $M$ is a function $F:\mathrm {T} M\to [0,\infty )$ such that:

1.  $F(x,my)=mF(x,y)$ for all $(x,y)$ in $\mathrm {T} M$ and all $m\geq 0$,
2.  $F$ is infinitely differentiable in $\mathrm {T} M\setminus \{0\}$,
3.  The vertical Hessian of $F^{2}$ is positive definite.

### Symplectic geometry

[Symplectic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_geometry) is the study of [symplectic manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_manifold). An **almost symplectic manifold** is a differentiable manifold equipped with a smoothly varying [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate) [skew-symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-symmetric_matrix) [bilinear form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_form) on each tangent space, i.e., a nondegenerate 2-[form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_form) *ω*, called the *symplectic form*. A symplectic manifold is an almost symplectic manifold for which the symplectic form *ω* is closed: d*ω* = 0.

A diffeomorphism between two symplectic manifolds which preserves the symplectic form is called a [symplectomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectomorphism). Non-degenerate skew-symmetric bilinear forms can only exist on even-dimensional vector spaces, so symplectic manifolds necessarily have even dimension. In dimension 2, a symplectic manifold is just a surface endowed with an area form and a symplectomorphism is an area-preserving diffeomorphism. The [phase space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space) of a mechanical system is a symplectic manifold and they made an implicit appearance already in the work of [Joseph Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) on [analytical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_mechanics) and later in [Carl Gustav Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacobi)'s and [William Rowan Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rowan_Hamilton)'s [formulations of classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics).

By contrast with Riemannian geometry, where the [curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature) provides a local invariant of Riemannian manifolds, [Darboux's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darboux%27s_theorem) states that all symplectic manifolds are locally isomorphic. The only invariants of a symplectic manifold are global in nature and topological aspects play a prominent role in symplectic geometry. The first result in symplectic topology is probably the [Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9%E2%80%93Birkhoff_theorem), conjectured by [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincar%C3%A9) and then proved by [G.D. Birkhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G.D._Birkhoff) in 1912. It claims that if an area preserving map of an [annulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annulus_(mathematics) "Annulus (mathematics)") twists each boundary component in opposite directions, then the map has at least two fixed points.

### Contact geometry

[Contact geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_geometry) deals with certain manifolds of odd dimension. It is close to symplectic geometry and like the latter, it originated in questions of classical mechanics. A *contact structure* on a (2*n* + 1)-dimensional manifold *M* is given by a smooth hyperplane field *H* in the [tangent bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_bundle) that is as far as possible from being associated with the level sets of a differentiable function on *M* (the technical term is "completely nonintegrable tangent hyperplane distribution"). Near each point *p*, a hyperplane distribution is determined by a nowhere vanishing [1-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_form) $\alpha$, which is unique up to multiplication by a nowhere vanishing function:

$H_{p}=\ker \alpha _{p}\subset T_{p}M.$

A local 1-form on *M* is a *contact form* if the restriction of its [exterior derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_derivative) to *H* is a non-degenerate two-form and thus induces a symplectic structure on *H*<sub>*p*</sub> at each point. If the distribution *H* can be defined by a global one-form $\alpha$ then this form is contact if and only if the top-dimensional form

$\alpha \wedge (d\alpha )^{n}$

is a [volume form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_form) on *M*, i.e. does not vanish anywhere. A contact analogue of the Darboux theorem holds: all contact structures on an odd-dimensional manifold are locally isomorphic and can be brought to a certain local normal form by a suitable choice of the coordinate system.

### Complex and Kähler geometry

*Complex differential geometry* is the study of [complex manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_manifolds). An [almost complex manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_complex_manifold) is a *real* manifold $M$, endowed with a [tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor) of type (1, 1), i.e. a [vector bundle endomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundle) (called an *[almost complex structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_complex_structure)*)

$J:TM\rightarrow TM$, such that $J^{2}=-1.\,$

It follows from this definition that an almost complex manifold is even-dimensional.

An almost complex manifold is called *complex* if $N_{J}=0$, where $N_{J}$ is a tensor of type (2, 1) related to $J$, called the [Nijenhuis tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nijenhuis_tensor) (or sometimes the *torsion*). An almost complex manifold is complex if and only if it admits a [holomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_function) [coordinate atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_(topology) "Atlas (topology)"). An *[almost Hermitian structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_manifold)* is given by an almost complex structure *J*, along with a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) *g*, satisfying the compatibility condition

$g(JX,JY)=g(X,Y).\,$

An almost Hermitian structure defines naturally a [differential two-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_form)

$\omega _{J,g}(X,Y):=g(JX,Y).\,$

The following two conditions are equivalent:

1.  $N_{J}=0{\mbox{ and }}d\omega =0\,$
2.  $\nabla J=0\,$

where $\nabla$ is the [Levi-Civita connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi-Civita_connection) of $g$. In this case, $(J,g)$ is called a *[Kähler structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%A4hler_manifold)*, and a *Kähler manifold* is a manifold endowed with a Kähler structure. In particular, a Kähler manifold is both a complex and a [symplectic manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_manifold). A large class of Kähler manifolds (the class of [Hodge manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_manifold)) is given by all the smooth [complex projective varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry).

### CR geometry

[CR geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CR_structure) is the study of the intrinsic geometry of boundaries of domains in [complex manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_manifold).

### Conformal geometry

[Conformal geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_geometry) is the study of the set of angle-preserving (conformal) transformations on a space.

### Differential topology

[Differential topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_topology) is the study of global geometric invariants without a metric or symplectic form.

Differential topology starts from the natural operations such as [Lie derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_derivative) of natural [vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundle) and [de Rham differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_derivative) of [forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_form). Beside [Lie algebroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebroid), also [Courant algebroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courant_algebroid) start playing a more important role.

### Lie groups

A [Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group) is a [group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics) "Group (mathematics)") in the category of smooth manifolds. Beside the algebraic properties this enjoys also differential geometric properties. The most obvious construction is that of a Lie algebra which is the tangent space at the unit endowed with the Lie bracket between left-invariant [vector fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_field). Beside the structure theory there is also the wide field of [representation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_a_Lie_group).

### Geometric analysis

[Geometric analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_analysis) is a mathematical discipline where tools from differential equations, especially elliptic partial differential equations are used to establish new results in differential geometry and differential topology.

### Gauge theory

Gauge theory is the study of connections on vector bundles and principal bundles, and arises out of problems in [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics) and physical [gauge theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory) which underpin the [standard model of particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_model_of_particle_physics). Gauge theory is concerned with the study of differential equations for connections on bundles, and the resulting geometric [moduli spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space) of solutions to these equations as well as the invariants that may be derived from them. These equations often arise as the [Euler–Lagrange equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%E2%80%93Lagrange_equations) describing the equations of motion of certain physical systems in [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory), and so their study is of considerable interest in physics.

## Bundles and connections

The apparatus of [vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundle), [principal bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_bundle), and [connections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connection_(mathematics) "Connection (mathematics)") on bundles plays an extraordinarily important role in modern differential geometry. A smooth manifold always carries a natural vector bundle, the [tangent bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_bundle). Loosely speaking, this structure by itself is sufficient only for developing analysis on the manifold, while doing geometry requires, in addition, some way to relate the tangent spaces at different points, i.e. a notion of [parallel transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_transport). An important example is provided by [affine connections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_connection). For a surface in **R**<sup>3</sup>, tangent planes at different points can be identified using a natural path-wise parallelism induced by the ambient Euclidean space, which has a well-known standard definition of metric and parallelism. In [[Riemannian Geometry]], the [Levi-Civita connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi-Civita_connection) serves a similar purpose. More generally, differential geometers consider spaces with a vector bundle and an arbitrary affine connection which is not defined in terms of a metric. In physics, the manifold may be [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime) and the bundles and connections are related to various physical fields.

## Intrinsic versus extrinsic

From the beginning and through the middle of the 19th century, differential geometry was studied from the *extrinsic* point of view: curves and surfaces were considered as lying in a Euclidean space of higher dimension (for example a surface in an [ambient space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_space) of three dimensions). The simplest results are those in the differential geometry of curves and differential geometry of surfaces. Starting with the work of [Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann), the *intrinsic* point of view was developed, in which one cannot speak of moving "outside" the geometric object because it is considered to be given in a free-standing way. The fundamental result here is Gauss's [theorema egregium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorema_egregium), to the effect that [Gaussian curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_curvature) is an intrinsic invariant.

The intrinsic point of view is more flexible. For example, it is useful in relativity where space-time cannot naturally be taken as extrinsic. However, there is a price to pay in technical complexity: the intrinsic definitions of curvature and [connections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connection_(mathematics) "Connection (mathematics)") become much less visually intuitive.

These two points of view can be reconciled, i.e. the extrinsic geometry can be considered as a structure additional to the intrinsic one. (See the [Nash embedding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_embedding_theorem).) In the formalism of [geometric calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_calculus) both extrinsic and intrinsic geometry of a manifold can be characterized by a single [bivector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bivector)-valued one-form called the [shape operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator).

## Applications

Below are some examples of how differential geometry is applied to other fields of science and mathematics.

- In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), differential geometry has many applications, including:
  - Differential geometry is the language in which [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein)'s [general theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_theory_of_relativity) is expressed. According to the theory, the universe is a smooth manifold equipped with a pseudo-Riemannian metric, which describes the curvature of [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime). Understanding this curvature is essential for the positioning of [satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellites) into orbit around the Earth. Differential geometry is also indispensable in the study of [gravitational lensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_lensing) and [black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_holes).
  - [Differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms) are used in the study of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism).
  - Differential geometry has applications to both [Lagrangian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_mechanics) and [Hamiltonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics). Symplectic manifolds in particular can be used to study [Hamiltonian systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_system).
  - Riemannian geometry and contact geometry have been used to construct the formalism of [geometrothermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrothermodynamics) which has found applications in classical equilibrium [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics).
- In [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) and [biophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysics) when modelling cell membrane structure under varying pressure.
- In [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), differential geometry has applications to the field of [econometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrics).
- [Geometric modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_modeling) (including [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics)) and [computer-aided geometric design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_geometric_design) draw on ideas from differential geometry.
- In [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), differential geometry can be applied to solve problems in [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing).
- In [probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability), [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics), and [information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory), one can interpret various structures as Riemannian manifolds, which yields the field of [information geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_geometry), particularly via the [Fisher information metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information_metric).
- In [structural geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_geology), differential geometry is used to analyze and describe geologic structures.
- In [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision), differential geometry is used to analyze shapes.
- In [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing), differential geometry is used to process and analyse data on non-flat surfaces.
- [Grigori Perelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Perelman)'s proof of the [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_conjecture) using the techniques of [Ricci flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_flow) demonstrated the power of the differential-geometric approach to questions in [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology) and it highlighted the important role played by its analytic methods.
- In [wireless communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless), [Grassmannian manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassmannian) are used for [beamforming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beamforming) techniques in [multiple antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIMO) systems.
- In [geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesy), for calculating distances and angles on the mean sea level surface of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth), modelled by an ellipsoid of revolution.
- In [neuroimaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroimaging) and [brain-computer interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain-computer_interface), symmetric positive definite (SPD) manifolds are used to model functional, structural, or electrophysiological connectivity matrices.
